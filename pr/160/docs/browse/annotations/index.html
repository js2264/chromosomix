Annotations and bookmarks

Everything you add to a map, beyond its contacts, is listed in the Annotations panel, the column left of the map. It starts folded: press 2 or its button in the header to open it. Its cards, from the top, are Contact maps, Tracks, Structures, Manual annotations, Metadata annotations and Bookmarks. The last two start folded, and a card that the open file cannot use is not shown at all.

Loading tracks from files is covered in Tracks and features and The genome browser, and models on the Structures card in 3D models beside the map. This page is about the marks you draw, the results an analysis leaves, the file’s own columns and bookmarks.

Your own marks

Section titled “Your own marks”

The Manual annotations card holds marks you draw yourself. Nothing on it comes from a file. There are three kinds:

  • a line marks one position, as a vertical rule, a horizontal one, or both (a crosshair);
  • an arrowhead marks one cell of the map, a pair of positions, on or off the diagonal. It is the usual mark for a loop;
  • a rectangle marks a block of contacts between two stretches: a domain on the diagonal, a corner peak off it, or a locus against a whole arm.

A line is a mark in one dimension. An arrowhead and a rectangle are marks in two.

Adding a mark

Section titled “Adding a mark”

The card has a form for each kind, headed Lines, Arrowheads and Rectangles. Each takes a mark in one of two ways.

  • Type it and press add, or Enter. Coordinates are 1-based and inclusive, as IGV and samtools write them, and you can paste them with commas, kb or Mb. A line takes one position and an axis: vertical, horizontal or both. An arrowhead takes a position for the rows and one for the columns, and a rectangle a stretch for each. Leave the second box empty for the same place on both axes.
  • Pick it on the map: press pick on map (lines and arrowheads) or draw on map (rectangles), then click the map, or drag across it for a rectangle. Hold Shift while you drag to lock the rectangle to the diagonal. The mode stays on for as many marks as you want, until you press done or Esc.

You can also right-click the map: under Mark this point the menu offers Vertical line, Horizontal line, Crosshair and Arrowhead, at the cell under the pointer. On a 3D model, Mark a bead, Mark a loop and a Shift-click on a bead make marks too (see 3D models beside the map).

Beside the 1D browser the buttons read pick on track and draw on track. A track has one axis, so what you place lands on the diagonal: a line becomes a crosshair, an arrowhead sits on the diagonal, and a rectangle is the square on it. A map opened afterwards shows the same marks.

Each new mark takes the next colour of the palette.

Where a mark is drawn

Section titled “Where a mark is drawn”

A mark is drawn on every picture at once: on each map, square or turned, on the lanes beside it, in a tracks cell, in the 1D browser and on a 3D model. On the tracks, a line is a rule across every lane, an arrowhead is a small triangle at each of its two places, and a rectangle’s stretch is washed over every lane. A mark never sits on the pixel it is about: an arrowhead stands off the cell it points at, so the cell stays visible.

Moving, hiding and removing marks

Section titled “Moving, hiding and removing marks”

Each row of the card shows the mark’s place. Press it to edit the place in boxes like the forms’. Enter, a click away, or a Tab past the row’s last box moves the mark and keeps its colour and its place in the list. Escape leaves it where it was. A box that does not read stays open with a red edge.

Each row has an eye. Pressed, the mark leaves every picture and stays on the card, its eye shut, to be pressed back. Over the rows, the MARKS heading says how many are hidden and has one eye for all of them. A is that eye from the keyboard, and the command palette lists it as Manual annotations, with on or off beside it.

  • A double-click on a row’s eye shows that mark alone. A second double-click brings back the others.
  • What the eye over the list brings back is what it hid. A mark you hid on its own stays hidden.
  • Every picture’s right-click menu has a Marks group: Hide this line, Hide this arrowhead or Hide this rectangle for the marks under the pointer, then Hide every mark, or Show the marks once none is drawn.

A figure saved while a mark is hidden is saved without it. The × on a row removes that mark, and remove all under the list clears every mark on this file.

Saving and loading marks

Section titled “Saving and loading marks”

The card’s Export row writes the marks as a file. Choose lines (BED) or arrowheads & rectangles (BEDPE), then press the save button beside it. The file holds every mark of that kind, hidden ones included.

Marks come back in through the Import section: drop a BED or a BEDPE anywhere on the card, or click Load marks to choose one. Each row is read on its own merits:

  • a BED row is a line, on the axis its name column says (vertical, horizontal or both), or a crosshair at the interval’s start when the name is anything else;
  • a BEDPE row is an arrowhead when both of its anchors are one base wide, as the export writes one, and a rectangle otherwise.

Each mark takes the next colour, since neither format carries one, and a mark already at the same place is not added twice. A line under the zone reports what happened, such as marks.bed: 2 added, 1 skipped. The same BED dropped on the map instead becomes a track.

What an analysis leaves

Section titled “What an analysis leaves”

A task in the Analysis panel leaves its results where you can draw and compare them: as rows of the Tracks card. The drawer says so in one sentence when the run finishes, such as 3 tracks added to the Tracks card in the Annotations panel, marked *.

A computed track is a track

Section titled “A computed track is a track”

A result with one value per bin, such as an insulation score, an E1 or a coverage, becomes a track like a loaded bigWig. It has the same colour, height, style, scale, limits, groups, edges and batch settings, and it can be drawn in the 1D browser and coloured onto a 3D model. Three things tell it apart:

  • a * after its name. Its tooltip says it was computed here, by which task and from what view;
  • a line under its name saying what made it;
  • in its settings (the chevron, Show this track’s settings), the exports and the view it was computed from. A computed track leaves as bigWig, bedGraph or TSV.

Run the task again and its rows get the new values in place, in the same lane and the same spot in your arrangement.

Two loaded tracks can be combined the same way: tick both on the Tracks card and choose ratio or difference under combine…. The new row is computed too, and marked *.

A called set has two displays

Section titled “A called set has two displays”

Peaks, boundaries, domains, A and B compartments, loops: a set of intervals or pairs that a task called arrives as one row. Its settings open on display, which puts it in one of two places:

  • 1D track: segments beside the map and in the 1D browser, each as wide as its call. For a set of pairs it reads arcs (1D track): an arc joining the two ends of every pair;
  • map overlay: on the map it was called from. A set of pairs is a ring round each pair. A set of places, such as boundaries or peaks, has a look beside the display, point (a dot on the diagonal) or short diagonal marker (a rule across the diagonal that leaves the diagonal itself uncovered). A set of stretches, such as domains or compartments, is drawn as the corner of each one’s square along the diagonal.

Each task chooses where its sets open. A set read against a lane opens as a track right under that lane: peaks under the coverage they were called on, boundaries under the insulation score, A and B under E1. A set that is a picture of the map, such as domains or loops, opens on the map. So a run of insulation that calls boundaries and the domains between them lands as three rows: the score, the boundaries under it, and the domains on the map. In the 1D browser every called set is drawn as a track, since there is no map for it to be on.

Each called interval carries a name in the BED’s fourth column, numbered in genomic order, such as A or B, peak_12, domain_3 or boundary_40. A lane can be coloured by those names.

A called set leaves as BED, or BEDPE for pairs, and as TSV. A set of pairs also offers anchors: both ends of every pair as a BED, each place once however many pairs share it, for a motif scan or an overlap with peaks. A set of pairs you loaded from a file offers its anchors too.

After a reload

Section titled “After a reload”

The values of a computed row are not kept: they can be megabytes. Its recipe is: the task, its parameters and the view it was computed from. After a reload the row keeps its colour, name, limits and place, says values not kept over the reload, and offers recompute. A saved workspace and a shared link carry the recipe the same way, so whoever opens them gets the analysis rather than a number to take on trust.

Opening the same file at another resolution keeps the computed rows and their values, on the bins they were computed on. Opening a different file leaves them behind.

The file’s own columns

Section titled “The file’s own columns”

Some files keep tables beside their contacts. A balanced cooler’s bins table holds the weights it was balanced with, and often more columns that other tools added. The Metadata annotations card draws those columns as lanes. Choose one from its menu, where columns are grouped by the table they come from, and press add. From then on it is a lane like any other.

  • A column of numbers is drawn as bars. A column of labels is drawn as runs of colour with each category’s name written in, from a fixed palette, with a legend.
  • A column that differs on nearly every row is an identifier and is not offered.
  • A column can be no finer than the view. On a view where one cell of the map is a whole sequence, a column of the bins table is listed but greyed, and a note under the menu says why. Where one cell covers several bins, it shows their mean (numbers) or their commonest value (labels).
  • TSV writes the column as the file wrote it: one row per unit of its table, under # lines naming the file, the table and the column. A bins column is written for the bins read so far, those the picture covers, and the file says which.

A metadata row comes back after a reload ready to draw, since its data is in the map that is already open. The card is hidden for a file that carries no such columns.

Bookmarks

Section titled “Bookmarks”

A bookmark is a place on the genome and the bin size it was found at. It does not keep the zoom, the colour scale or which way the map was turned: those are how the picture was drawn, not where it is.

There are three ways to make one:

  • press the bookmark button beside go in the map’s title bar (Bookmark this view — the place on screen, at this resolution), or B, or save this view on the card. The card opens so you can see the new row;
  • type a window or a single position into the card’s Save a place box and press add. It reads coordinates as every locus box does, 1-based and inclusive, so a locus copied from IGV, UCSC or a paper lands where it says;
  • under Import, press regions from BED…. Every interval in the file becomes a bookmark: the first three columns are the place and the fourth, if there is one, its name. A BED states no bin size, so these open at whatever resolution the map is at.

Each row shows the name if it has one, the place, and the resolution it was saved at. Click the row to go there: the whole sequence opens, zoomed to the place, so you can pan to either side of it. The × forgets one place and remove all forgets every place on this file. Saving a place that is already there says so instead of adding it twice.

On a 3D model, bookmarks are written at their middle beads, with the sequence names.

Taking annotations out

Section titled “Taking annotations out”
What Where Formats
Your marks Manual annotations, Export lines as BED; arrowheads and rectangles as BEDPE
A computed track its row’s settings, or the Analysis drawer bigWig, bedGraph, TSV
A called set its row’s settings, or the Analysis drawer BED, or BEDPE for pairs, and TSV. A set of pairs’ anchors from its row
A loaded set of pairs its row’s settings anchors, as BED
A metadata column its row on Metadata annotations TSV
A 3D model its row on Structures bed3d, PDB

The bigWig is written by chromosomix itself, with no conversion step, for IGV, deepTools and pyBigWig. A bedGraph has one line per bin that has a value: bins with none are left out, never written as 0. A TSV opens with the task, its parameters and the view as # lines, which a bigWig has no room for. Bookmarks have no export of their own: they travel in a saved workspace and a shared link.

Every file goes through your system’s save dialog, where the browser has one, and is listed in the console. See Figures and export.

What is kept, and where

Section titled “What is kept, and where”

Every track, mark, bookmark and setting is remembered for its file in this tab. It survives a reload, a change of resolution, a switch between the map and the tracks, and a deck opening and closing. Two tabs on the same file share none of it.

The link in the address bar carries only what is being looked at, not these. A link made with Share this tab as a link, and a saved workspace, carry them all; see Sessions, workspaces and links.

After a reload, each row comes back as its kind allows:

  • a track from an address is read again from there;
  • a track from your disk keeps every setting and asks for the file again, with re-open this file, or allow access where this browser kept permission to read it. Dropping the same file again answers it, rather than adding a second row;
  • a computed row comes back with recompute;
  • a metadata row comes back ready.
