P(s) averages every bin of a sequence into one curve and answers how a genome folds; the scalogram keeps
the bins apart and answers where it folds differently from itself. At each bin it takes the distribution
of the bin's contacts over separation and reports the separation within which a given share of them lies
— a tenth, a quarter, a half, three quarters, nine tenths — drawn along the genome as nested bands. It is
Lioy et al. (2018)'s reading, the standard picture of where a bacterial chromosome's fold is tight and
where it is loose, and the quantity HiContacts' `scalogram()` draws. Reach for it when a decay curve has
made you wonder which part of the chromosome carries its bump.

## Running it

Open a contact map, then choose **Scalogram** in the **2D tasks** card of the **Analysis** panel. Tick
the sequences, choose the quantiles, and press **run**. With **look out to** at 0, the
default, each sequence is read out to its own length, which is the whole square around it and is what the
price says: quick on a bacterial or a yeast map, and a large read on a mammalian chromosome, where a reach
reads a band along the diagonal instead.

The bands arrive on the **Tracks** card of the **Annotations** panel, a row each — *Scalogram 10 %*,
*Scalogram 25 %* and so on — grouped as **Scalogram** in one lane, where each band fills down to the one
below it. The lane's axis is logarithmic, in base pairs, with a faint rule at each power of ten.

## Parameters

**look out to** is the one to think about. It is a number of bins, the depth of the band the run reads,
and 0 reads each sequence whole: the only setting at which a band is a quantile of all of a bin's
contacts. Any other reach makes every band a quantile of what lies within it, and a different number
under the same name; so the run's note says how far the reach is in base pairs, and every band's name
carries it, as in *Scalogram 50 % · within 500 kb*. **diagonals to ignore** leaves out each bin's contact
with itself, the largest number in its row and not a contact at a distance, which would pull every band
towards zero; **minimum coverage of the distribution** leaves a bin without bands where too few of its
separations have a value.

## Reading the result

Read the lane as one shape. Each band is a separation in base pairs: at the 50 % band, half of everything
the bin touches lies closer than the band's height. A low, narrow bundle is a stretch whose contacts are
local; a high one is a stretch whose contacts reach far along the chromosome; and a step in every band at
the same place is a boundary between two ways of folding.

A quantile rather than a mean, because contact has a heavy tail: the mean separation of a bin's contacts
moves by a factor of two when the balancer drops one distant cell, and its median does not, which keeps a
deep map and a shallow one comparable. Each quantile is interpolated between the two separations it falls
between, so the bands are smooth rather than stepped at multiples of the bin size.

A hole in the bands is a bin with too little of its distribution to measure, beside a stretch the
balancer dropped for instance; a band exists only where both its own curve and the one below it do.

## Things to know

- **The bands' order is the picture.** Each band fills down to the one below it in the lane, so the order
  of the rows in the group is the order they nest in: reorder them and the picture changes with them.
- **Circular sequences.** On a sequence declared circular, a separation is the shorter of the two ways
  round and reaches at most half the sequence; the run's note counts such sequences.
- **Short sequences**, of fewer than four bins, have no distribution of separations and are left out,
  with a note.
- **The values are base pairs.** The logarithmic axis is only how the lane is drawn: the bigWig, the
  bedGraph and the TSV hold the separations as computed.
- **Not yet validated.** The scalogram follows Lioy et al., but the [validation](/analyses/validation/)
  does not yet compare it with a reference, so its badge is not green.
